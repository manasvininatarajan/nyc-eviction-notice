NYC Tenant Crisis Guide

I Just Got an Eviction Notice.
What Do I Do?

You have rights. You have time. You do not have to leave just because you got a piece of paper.

⚠️ Do not ignore the notice — but do not panic either. Read this first.

Get a free lawyer — right now

NYC law gives most tenants a free attorney in Housing Court. You do not need to pay anything. Call your borough's Legal Aid office today.

The most important thing you need to know: A notice is NOT an eviction. Your landlord cannot remove you, change your locks, or shut off your utilities without a court order. Only a city marshal can carry out an eviction — and only after a judge rules against you in Housing Court. You have time to act.
1
Figure out what kind of notice you received
📄 Non-Payment Notice ("14-Day Rent Demand")

Your landlord says you owe rent. You have 14 days to pay or reach an agreement before they can file in court. This is the most common notice and the most solvable — see Step 3 for emergency rental assistance.

📄 Notice of Petition (court papers)

Your landlord has already filed in Housing Court. You will have a court date printed on the papers. You must show up to that date — if you don't, the judge can rule against you automatically. Call Legal Aid today.

📄 Holdover Notice

Your landlord says you have no right to be in the apartment — maybe your lease ended, or they claim another reason. This is more complex. Get a lawyer immediately.

Not sure which one you have? Call Housing Court Answers at 718-557-1379 or 212-962-4795 Monday–Friday 9am–5pm. They will help you understand your papers for free.

4
Gather your documents now

Before you call anyone, collect as much of this as you can. Having it ready speeds everything up.

  • The eviction notice or court papers you received
  • Your lease or rental agreement
  • Recent pay stubs, benefit letters, or proof of income
  • Any rent payment receipts or bank records showing you paid
  • Any written communication with your landlord (texts, emails, letters)
  • Photo ID (any form — immigration status does not matter)
  • Your Housing Court case number (if you have one)
Don't have all of these? Go anyway. Legal aid organizations are used to helping people who don't have everything. Missing documents won't stop them from helping you.
5
Know your rights
Can my landlord lock me out or turn off my utilities? ▼
No. This is illegal in NYC. Your landlord cannot change your locks, remove your belongings, shut off heat, water, or electricity, or harass you to make you leave — even if you owe rent. If this happens, call 311 immediately and contact Legal Aid. This is called an "illegal lockout" and you have the right to return to your apartment.
Do I have to leave when I get the notice? ▼
No. A notice is the first step in a legal process — not the end of it. Your landlord must go to Housing Court, and a judge must rule against you before any eviction can happen. Even after a ruling, only a city marshal can carry out the eviction, and you will receive advance notice. This process typically takes months.
What if I don't have immigration papers? ▼
Your immigration status does not affect your right to legal help in Housing Court. NYC's Right to Counsel and all legal aid organizations serve tenants regardless of immigration status. You cannot be asked about your status in Housing Court.
What if I miss my court date? ▼
This is serious. If you miss your first Housing Court appearance, the judge can issue a default judgment against you — meaning your landlord wins automatically. Call Legal Aid immediately if you missed a date. It may not be too late to file a motion to reopen the case, but you need to act fast.
Is my apartment rent stabilized? ▼
If your building has 6 or more units and was built before 1974, you may be rent stabilized. Rent stabilized tenants have stronger protections against eviction and rent increases. To check your status, call DHCR at 718-739-6400 or visit the NYC Rent Guidelines Board at rentguidelinesboard.cityofnewyork.us, or call 212-669-7480.
What is Good Cause Eviction? ▼
As of April 2024, NYC's Good Cause Eviction Law gives many tenants in non-rent-stabilized apartments new protections against large rent increases and eviction without good reason. There are exemptions — small landlords, high-rent units, and buildings built after 2009 may not be covered. Ask your lawyer whether this law applies to you.
